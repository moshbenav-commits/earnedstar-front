# What actually counts as a "verified purchase" review

**Quick answer.** "Verified purchase" should mean one thing: the review is tied
to a confirmed, completed order between that reviewer and that seller. Many
platforms stretch the label to cover looser signals — a logged-in account, an
email match, a self-reported claim — that confirm far less. That gap
determines how much weight the badge should actually carry.

## What does "verified purchase" actually require?

At minimum, three things have to line up: a real order record, a specific
product on that order, and a reviewer identity matched to the person who placed
it. If any one of those is missing, the review might still be genuine, but it
isn't verified in the sense the label implies.

This is a narrower bar than it sounds. It rules out reviews from someone who
received the product as a gift, a sample, or a resale, unless the platform can
trace the transaction back to an actual purchase. It also rules out a review
submitted by anyone simply willing to click "I bought this."

## Why do platforms use the label so inconsistently?

Because the strict version is expensive to build and the loose version isn't.

Matching a review to a real order requires order data — something a review
platform only has if it's actually wired into checkout or fulfillment systems.
Plenty of review tools operate independently of that data, so they substitute
something easier to check: an email address that matches a receipt someone
uploaded, an account that's simply logged in, or nothing more than a checkbox
the reviewer ticks themselves. All of it gets called "verified," and the badge
looks identical regardless of which standard produced it.

The result is a word that's stopped doing the job it's supposed to do. A
shopper who trusts the badge is trusting a claim they have no way to audit.

## What's the difference between a verified badge and an honest review?

Verification and honesty solve two different problems, and conflating them is
where most of the confusion starts.

Verification answers: did this transaction happen? Honesty answers: is this
review an accurate account of that experience? A review can be fully verified
and still be shaped by an incentive, written under pressure from a support
interaction, or softened because the reviewer didn't want to seem difficult.
None of that shows up in a badge that only checks for a matched order.

| Question | What verification answers | What it doesn't answer |
|---|---|---|
| Did a real transaction occur? | Yes, if matched to order data | — |
| Did this specific person buy it? | Yes, if identity is matched | — |
| Is the review's content accurate? | Not addressed | Requires separate moderation |
| Was the review incentivized? | Not addressed | Requires disclosure policy |

A serious review program needs both layers: verification at collection, and a
separate set of rules — no incentives tied to sentiment, no filtering by
expected rating — governing what happens after.

## How can a merchant check what their own platform's badge really means?

Ask the platform one direct question: what specific data point triggers the
verified label? If the answer traces back to an actual order record matched to
the reviewer, the badge means what shoppers assume it means. If the answer is
softer — a logged-in account, a self-reported claim, an email that merely looks
plausible — the badge is decoration, and shoppers are trusting something the
platform can't actually back up.

It's worth checking this even on a platform already in use. A badge inherited
from a default configuration, never audited, can end up meaning far less than
whoever set it up assumed.

## What should a shopper look for beyond the badge itself?

Specificity. A verified badge on a review that says "great product, love it"
carries less useful signal than an unverified review that describes exact fit,
use case, or a specific flaw. The badge confirms the transaction; it doesn't
confirm the review is worth reading.

The two checks work together, not as substitutes for each other — a verified,
specific review is the strongest signal a shopper can act on, and neither
check alone gets there.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "verified purchase" mean, in the strictest sense?**
A review tied to a confirmed, completed transaction between the specific
reviewer and the specific seller — not a purchase somewhere else, not an
account that merely exists, and not a claim the platform never checked.

**Does a verified badge guarantee the review is honest?**
No. Verification confirms a transaction happened. It says nothing about
whether the review was incentivized, coached, or written by someone other
than the buyer. Verification and honesty are separate problems solved by
separate controls.

**Why do so many platforms use "verified" loosely?**
Because a looser standard is cheaper to build and produces more reviews
faster. Confirming a real, matched transaction requires order data most open
platforms never collect, so many settle for weaker signals — an email
confirmation, a logged-in account — and label the result "verified" anyway.

**What should a merchant check before trusting a "verified" badge on their own
review platform?**
Ask what specific data point triggers the label. If the answer is anything
short of "a completed order record matched to this reviewer," the badge is
marketing language, not a verification claim a shopper should be able to
rely on.

# Verified-purchase gates versus open review platforms

**Quick answer.** A gated review platform only accepts reviews from customers
matched to a confirmed order. An open platform accepts reviews from anyone
with an account, purchase or not. The difference isn't presentation — it
determines whether a star rating reflects people who actually bought the
product or a mix that includes people who never did.

## What does "gated" actually mean, mechanically?

It means the review form is locked behind a real check: does this reviewer's
identity match an order record for this specific product? If the match
fails, the review either doesn't get submitted or gets flagged and held for
review before it publishes.

That's a narrower, harder standard than it might sound like from the outside.
It excludes reviews from people who researched the product but bought
elsewhere, people who received it as a gift with no order record under their
name, and anyone submitting a review with no transaction behind it at all.

## What does "open" actually mean, and why do platforms build it that way?

Open means the review form accepts a submission from any account in good
standing, with no purchase check at all. Some open platforms verify identity
loosely — an email, a login — but none of that confirms a transaction
happened.

Platforms build it this way because it's simpler and it scales faster. No
order data integration is required, no matching logic, no handling for the
edge cases a real purchase check runs into (returns, gifts, marketplace
fulfillment). The trade-off is that "review" on an open platform can mean
anything from a detailed account of ownership to a comment from someone who
never bought the product at all.

## What does each approach actually get a merchant?

| | Gated (verified-purchase) | Open |
|---|---|---|
| Who can review | Matched, confirmed buyers only | Anyone with an account |
| Volume | Lower, especially early | Higher, faster to accumulate |
| Fake-review resistance | Structurally harder to fake | Easier to flood or manipulate |
| Pre-purchase research feedback | Not captured | Captured |
| Credibility if challenged | Defensible — backed by order data | Harder to defend on a specific review |
| Build complexity | Requires order-data integration | Minimal |

Neither column is universally correct. A gated system produces a smaller,
more defensible set of reviews. An open system produces a larger set faster,
at the cost of not being able to say with confidence that every review came
from someone who bought the product.

## Why does the gate matter more than it looks like it should?

Because a star rating is only as trustworthy as what's allowed to contribute
to it.

An average built from confirmed buyers describes real product experience. An
average that also includes reviews from people who never purchased —
competitors, people who browsed and left, reviews submitted in bad faith —
describes something else, even if most of the individual reviews inside it
are genuine. The gate is what determines which population the number
actually represents.

Regulators have started treating this distinction as more than a nicety:
guidance on consumer reviews increasingly draws a hard line around reviews
from people who never had a genuine experience with the product, regardless
of platform.

## Can a platform call itself "gated" without the gate meaning much?

Yes, and this is worth checking directly rather than assuming. "Gated" is
only as strong as what it's checking. A platform that gates on an
uploaded receipt image nobody verifies, or a self-reported "I bought this"
checkbox, has added a step that resembles verification without providing it.
The label alone doesn't tell you which kind you're looking at — the specific
check behind the label does.

## How should a merchant decide which approach fits?

Match the gate to what the reviews are meant to prove. If the goal is a
defensible signal that a real customer stands behind the product, a genuine
verified-purchase gate, checked against real order data, is the only version
that actually delivers that. If the goal is broad discussion and research
feedback ahead of purchase, an open system captures something a gated one
structurally can't. Most serious review programs end up needing both — a
gated core for the trust signal, and separate, clearly labeled channels for
everything else.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the actual mechanical difference between a gated and an open
platform?**
A gated platform only accepts a review after matching the reviewer to a
completed order for that specific product. An open platform accepts a
review from anyone with an account, regardless of whether they bought
anything at all.

**Why would a merchant choose an open platform at all?**
Volume and reach. An open platform collects reviews faster because there's
no purchase to wait for, and it can absorb reviews from people researching a
product before buying — feedback a purely gated system never sees.

**Does gating reviews behind a verified purchase reduce the total number
collected?**
Usually, yes, at least early on. The trade is fewer total reviews for a set
that's harder to fake, easier to defend if challenged, and more directly
tied to real product experience.

**Can a platform claim to be gated without actually verifying anything
meaningful?**
Yes. "Gated" only means what it's actually checking against. A platform
that gates on a self-reported claim or an unverified receipt upload is not
meaningfully different from an open one — it just adds a step that looks
like verification without providing it.

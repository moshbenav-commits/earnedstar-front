# What to do about a negative review that's factually accurate

**Quick answer.** A negative review that's also true can't be argued away or
removed — only acknowledged and acted on. The right response confirms the
specific problem and states what's being done about it, without disputing
how the customer felt. The fix that matters most happens after the response,
in whether the same complaint shows up again.

## Why is an accurate negative review harder to handle than a false one?

Because there's no factual ground to push back on.

A false review — one describing a purchase that never happened, or an
experience invented outright — can usually be flagged, reported, or contested
with the platform directly. An accurate review leaves none of those options
open. The shipment really was late. The product really did arrive with a
crack. Disputing an accurate account only makes the response look like denial,
which reads worse to everyone watching than the original complaint did.

## What's the first thing to check before responding?

Whether the review is actually accurate, not whether it's fair.

Accuracy and fairness are different questions. A review can be completely
accurate and still feel unfair — a customer describing a two-day shipping
delay as "the worst experience of my life" is technically reporting something
real, even if the framing is dramatic. The response should engage with the
factual core, not the tone. Arguing about the tone is a losing move; it
signals defensiveness instead of ownership.

## What should the public response actually contain?

Three things, in order: acknowledgment, specifics, and a concrete next step.

- **Acknowledge the specific issue**, by name, not a vague "we're sorry you
  had a bad experience." A response that names the actual problem shows the
  merchant read the review rather than pasted a template.
- **State what's being done about it** — a refund, a replacement, a process
  change — in terms specific enough that a future shopper reading the thread
  understands the problem is being taken seriously, not just apologized for.
- **Avoid re-litigating the account.** Even where the merchant's version of
  events differs slightly, a public reply is not the venue to argue the
  details. Move that conversation to a direct channel and keep the public
  response focused on ownership.

| Response pattern | What it signals | Effect on future shoppers |
|---|---|---|
| Generic apology, no specifics | Templated, disengaged | Reads as damage control |
| Specific acknowledgment + fix offered | Taken seriously, handled | Reads as a company that responds |
| Disputing the reviewer's account publicly | Defensive | Reads worse than the original complaint |
| No response at all | Unaddressed | Reads as either absent or indifferent |

## Should a merchant try to get an accurate negative review taken down?

No. Most review platforms won't remove a review for being unflattering, and
pursuing removal anyway — through repeated appeals, through pressure on the
customer, through disputing verified purchase status that isn't actually in
question — tends to surface publicly and reads far worse than the original
review ever did.

The review staying up isn't the problem to solve. What it describes is.

## Does the response matter more than actually fixing the problem?

No — and this is the part easiest to get backwards. A well-crafted response
to a shipping delay that happens again next month accomplishes nothing. The
response buys a small amount of goodwill in the moment; whether that goodwill
holds depends entirely on whether the next batch of reviews shows the same
complaint or a different one.

Treat every accurate negative review as a free, specific bug report. The
response handles the immediate relationship. The fix is what actually changes
what future reviews say.

## How should a merchant follow up after responding?

Check whether the fix worked, using the next round of reviews as the test.

If the same specific complaint — the same defect, the same delay, the same
support failure — shows up again from a different customer, the underlying
issue wasn't actually addressed, regardless of how the first response read.
That recurrence is more informative than the original review was on its own,
because it confirms whether the acknowledgment was followed by a real change
or just a well-worded reply.

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes a negative review "factually accurate" versus just harsh?**
An accurate review describes something that actually happened — a late
shipment, a defect, a size that ran small, a support call that went badly —
even if the tone is blunt. It's distinct from a false or malicious review,
which describes something that didn't happen at all.

**Should a merchant ever ask to have an accurate negative review removed?**
No. Asking a platform to remove a true review because it's unflattering is a
request most review platforms will refuse, and pursuing it anyway damages
trust further if the customer or platform makes the attempt visible.

**What should the public response actually say?**
Acknowledge the specific issue without minimizing it, state what's being
done about it, and avoid arguing with the reviewer's account of events —
even the parts that sting. The response is being read by every future
shopper, not just the original reviewer.

**Does fixing the underlying problem matter more than the response itself?**
Yes. A well-written response to a problem that recurs next month is worse
than a plain response to a problem that actually gets fixed, because the
next round of reviews will show whether anything changed.
